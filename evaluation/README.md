# Evaluation evidence for OpenSidewalks NYC

Most of the evidence here was produced on v0.3.3. v0.3.4 did not change geometry, incline or routing inputs. v0.3.5 writes plazas and other pedestrian areas as Pedestrian Zones in place of Edges along their outlines, so it has its own validator result, post-build checks, comparison with v0.3.4 and reachability figures: the files in [`v0.3.5/`](v0.3.5/), which its own README lists. The crossing rule check, the bridge diagnosis, the structure heights and the router comparison were not rerun on v0.3.5.

This folder holds the evidence behind the claims made for the graph and for the comparison with other routers: protocols, raw ratings, result files and a few example images. Each section below says what was claimed, how it was tested, the result, where the files are and which tracked code produced it. The section after that lists what the evidence does not show.

## How the evidence was made

The work was done on one machine from public data. Result files were copied here from the project's working archive, which is not published. Some files still name paths in that archive (`research_notes/...`, `sources/`, `raw/`, scripts such as `diagnose.py`); those files are not here unless a section below says so. The scripts that drew the samples and computed the scores are copied as they ran into `code/` folders beside their results (`crossing_rule/code/`, `reachability/code/`, `compare/results/code/`, `compare/disagreements/code/`). Their input and output paths point into the archive, so they document the method and need their paths changed to run. The exception is the crossing rule score, which recomputes from published files (section 3). The pipeline, the converters and the comparison runners are tracked in this repository and are named in each section.

Language models did much of the work. The raters in every imagery check were Anthropic Claude models, run as separate instances through Claude Code. Each instance was launched with the written protocol and its sheets or images. The protocol files in this folder (`crossing_rule/PROTOCOL.md`, `compare/disagreements/PROTOCOL.md`) are the instructions the raters received, with the later edits listed in each file's note. The message that launched each instance and the instances' transcripts are not published. The exact model versions were not recorded. Agreement between instances measures how consistent the model is, not how accurate it is, because instances of one model can share the same errors. No person rated any sheet, crossing or route image, and nothing was checked on the ground. This applies to the 200 crossing sheets, the 48 disagreement sheets and the landmark route images (`reachability/hand_check.md`). Claude instances also wrote the bridge diagnosis and drafted these notes. Where a copied file says "I", it is one of those instances writing.

The author designed and directed this work, chose the questions, the samples and the rules, and reviewed and accepted the results. The author did not rate any imagery.

Copies were edited only to remove local paths, to replace working wording with the disclosure above, and to add dated notes where a number had since changed or a statement was corrected. Every edit is marked as a note or is a wording change; no computed value was changed.

## Evidence lines

### 1. The graph is valid and complete

Claim: v0.3.3 validates under `python-osw-validation` 0.5.0 and was built in one run from an empty cache. Test: the validator and every post-build check on the final build at commit `2442db2`. Result: valid with 0 errors, 4,068,058 features (`build/validator.json`, `build/checks.json`). Every edge difference from v0.3.2 is sorted into the commit that explains it (`build/compare_v032.json`). Code: `validators/post_build_checks.py`.

### 2. Incline on bridges and elevated ways

Claim: deck heights from the LiDAR point clouds put incline on structures. Test: the 2017 heights against the 2014 USGS survey, which the fix did not use for those nodes, and the lifted nodes against the city's planimetric transport structure polygons. Result: 14,402 nodes carry a deck height and 98.5% of bridge edges have an incline (`build/checks.json`); 83.4% of 2017 heights have a 2014 surface within 0.25 m and 91.6% within 1 m (`structure_incline/structure_validate_2014.json`); 73.6% of nodes lifted 2 m or more lie within 5 m of a structure polygon (`structure_incline/structure_where_check.json`). Footway edges over the wheelchair limits fell from 3.24% to 1.40% under 2 m long (`build/steep_by_length.json`). Code: `pipeline/utils/ept.py`, `pipeline/utils/deck.py`, `pipeline/stages/assemble.py`, `tests/test_structure_incline.py`; method in `METHODOLOGY.md` section 5b.

### 3. The 5 m ramp to crossing rule

Claim: a crossing end counts as ramped when a surveyed ramp is within 5 m, and this rule is right where it passes a crossing. Test: 200 crossings, 40 per borough (seed 20261002), each end rated over the city's 2018 orthoimagery by four language-model raters following `crossing_rule/PROTOCOL.md`, with every third sheet rated again by a fifth instance. The rated truth is what the 2018 aerial imagery and the survey positions show to those raters, not a check on the ground.

Result: of the 166 crossings the 5 m rule calls ramped, 164 were rated ramped. Precision is 0.988 (95% exact interval 0.957 to 0.999), so the false pass rate is 1.2% (2 of 166; 95% interval 0.15% to 4.3%). Recall is 1.0 (164 of 164; 95% interval 0.978 to 1.000), but that follows partly from how the sample and the truth were defined: a rater could only say yes where a surveyed ramp sits at the crosswalk, so recall cannot detect a crossing refused because the survey missed a ramp. A narrower rule, a surveyed ramp within 3 m, had no false pass and missed 5; with 2 errors against 0 this sample cannot tell the two rules apart, and the 5 m rule was kept because it was in place and leaves more routes (`crossing_rule/RESULT.md`, "Decision"). Agreement between instances was kappa 0.805 over three classes. The strict rule finds 0.555 of crossings rated ramped (`crossing_rule/score.json`). The intervals were computed on 2026-10-03 from the counts in `score.json`.

Recompute: `uv run python evaluation/crossing_rule/code/score.py evaluation/crossing_rule/sample/features.json evaluation/crossing_rule/sample/ratings OUT.json` reproduces `score.json` exactly. `sample/features.json` was added on 2026-10-03 for this. The step before it (`code/features.py`) needs the v0.3.2 graph tables, which are not published. Files: `crossing_rule/sample/` (the sample, its metadata, the per-end features, five rating files, three example sheets). Code: `crossings_with_ramps` in `scripts/osw_to_unweaver.py`, `tests/test_crossing_rule.py`; rule in `METHODOLOGY.md`, "Routing layer". The sampling frame is the v0.3.2 graph's crossings (`sample/sample_meta.json`); the rule did not change in v0.3.3.

### 4. Reachability on the final build

Claim: how often the wheelchair profile finds a route between pairs of graph nodes drawn at random. Test: 2,000 seeded random pairs per borough, drawn from nodes on walkable edges, checked from the drawn node. Node density weights the sample, so finely mapped areas count for more, and trip lengths follow node pairs, not trips people make. Result: wheelchair profile 84.8% Brooklyn, 63.0% Queens, 53.0% Manhattan, 43.7% Bronx, 43.1% Staten Island, each with a 95% interval in `reach.json` no wider than plus or minus 2.2 points (Brooklyn 83.2% to 86.3%); pedestrian edges alone 94.0, 73.3, 79.3, 74.2, 66.3; the Brooklyn Bridge to DUMBO route is 3,769 m (`reachability/reach.json`). Files: `reachability/hand_check.md` (ten routes described over imagery by a language-model rater, text only) and `reachability/blocked_on_routes.json` (the edges outside the limits on each landmark route). `blocked_on_routes.json` was regenerated on the final build for this folder. The per-edge figures the v0.3.3 report quotes hold on it: on the Queensboro Bridge a 17.5 m edge spans 11.1 m and the outer roadway reads 8.8%, and the Macombs Dam Bridge's Bronx ramp reads 13% to 19%. These steep values come from this pipeline's LiDAR heights at the edges' nodes, not from OSM tags. Code: `reachability/code/reach.py` (paths point into the archive). See "Two sets of reachability numbers" below before comparing these with section 6.

### 5. Bridges

Claim: each of the five bridges that v0.3.2 did not join has a known cause. Test: each bridge followed node by node in the raw extract and in the built graph, by a language-model instance. Result, as corrected on 2026-10-03: two were the test's own anchors. One (145th Street) follows from this graph not reading OSM's `sidewalk=*` tags on the street, where OSM records the sidewalks. One (Broadway) is OSM geometry, a missing crossing way and an unjoined corner, and the missing crossing is unconfirmed. One (the RFK Bronx span) is this pipeline's rule that drops cycleways with no `foot` tag, which is stricter than the United States default. Admitting every untagged cycleway would add 627 ways (`bridges/FINDINGS.md`, `bridges/findings.json`). On the final build 20 of 23 bridges are joined on pedestrian edges and 3 only along the street centreline (`bridges/bridges.json`). No OSM edit was made or proposed upstream.

### 6. Comparison with OpenRouteService, Valhalla and Unweaver

Claim: this graph finds fewer wheelchair routes than the routers people know, and refuses crossings with no surveyed ramp, slopes over its limits and street centrelines. The other routers do not have the ramp survey or the LiDAR incline. Given them (arm B, strict), ORS passes no kerb that the conversion tags as raised, but the audit still flags a crossing with no surveyed ramp on 14.1% of its Brooklyn routes and up to 44.5% on Staten Island. On every one of those routes ORS used a piece of a crossing whose own nodes carry no raised kerb, and on 94.8% of them it joined or left that piece along a street centreline, which arm B includes and this graph's wheelchair profile refuses (`compare/results/arm_b_crossings.json`). At its incline limit of 10, ORS also allows slopes between 8.3% and 10% that this profile refuses. Test: `compare/PROTOCOL.md`; 12,437 pairs on the same OSM extract, engines built from `../engines/`.

Configuration. ORS 10.0.1 ran its wheelchair profile with its recommended weighting, an incline limit of 10, a kerb limit of 0.06 m, `kerbs_on_crossings: true` and elevation off. In the v10.0.1 source its incline limit reads only the OSM `incline` tag, so elevation off does not change which routes it allows. Valhalla 3.9.0 ran pedestrian costing with `type: wheelchair`, without elevation tiles (so `use_hills` had nothing to act on) and with the default `sidewalk_factor`. That type excludes ways tagged `wheelchair=no` and surfaces rougher than `compacted`, penalises steps without forbidding them, and neither penalises nor excludes escalators. It is a baseline that avoids stairs, not a wheelchair router. `compare/results/tables.md` has a key to every row.

Result: this graph's wheelchair profile finds a route for 91.2% of Brooklyn pairs against 98.8% for ORS on plain OSM; 87.6% of ORS's Brooklyn routes use a crossing with no surveyed ramp within 5 m of an end (`compare/results/comparison.json`, `compare/results/tables.md`). The audit rows judge every router by this dataset's ramp survey and LiDAR, so this graph's wheelchair profile scores 0% on them by construction, and this graph's own walking profile scores 90.8% on "any of the four" in Brooklyn against 91.3% for ORS. "Roadway" in those rows means a street centreline, including centrelines where OSM tags a sidewalk on the street. Of 9,066 disagreements over the five boroughs, 61.9% come from kerb data and 21.7% from incline data (`compare/results/tables.md`, "cause" rows). Given this graph's own edges, ramp state and incline (arm C), ORS and this graph agree on whether a route exists for 88.75% to 93.35% of pairs in the six random samples, 92.95% in Brooklyn (`compare/results/arm_c.json`). That agreement counts pairs neither router finds (850 of 2,000 in the Bronx, 854 on Staten Island). In Brooklyn the two disagree on 141 of 2,000 pairs, 52 found only by this graph and 89 only by ORS, and the chance-corrected agreement computed from those counts is kappa 0.51.

Ferries. This graph has no ferry edges. 349 of the 2,000 Manhattan pairs have one end on Governors, Liberty or Ellis Island and the other elsewhere, and 698 of the 2,000 city-wide pairs have their ends on different lands, mostly Staten Island and the rest of the city. No walking route inside the city joins those lands, so this graph finds none of them, while the engines ride a ferry: ORS on 367 of its 1,954 Manhattan routes and 755 of 1,957 city-wide, Valhalla's wheelchair type on 666 of 1,883 and 1,253 of 1,933. With those pairs set apart, this graph's wheelchair profile finds 77.5% of Manhattan pairs, not 64.0%, and 69.4% city-wide, not 45.1%, against 97.3% and 97.6% for ORS. The same pairs are why ORS run on this graph with no limits completes 82.1% of Manhattan pairs: of the 357 it loses against plain OSM, 348 used a ferry there (`compare/results/ferries.json`; tables in `compare/results/tables.md`, "Ferries and pairs across water").

A small part of the gap is how ORS reads OSM. On the tag probe fixture, ORS 10.0.1 lets a crossing tagged `kerb=raised` pass every kerb limit (`compare/probes/ors_tag_probe.json`). This has not been reported to the ORS project. OSM tags 675 of the 15,599 crossing ends with no surveyed ramp within 5 m `kerb=raised` (4.3%; `compare/results/kerb_crosscheck.json`), so the tag marks few of the crossings this graph refuses. Counted by node, 51.2% of the reference's Brooklyn routes pass over a node tagged `kerb=raised` on a crossing they use. So do 27.4% of this graph's own wheelchair routes and 47.9% of its walking routes, because this graph takes kerbs from the ramp survey and does not read OSM kerb tags either (`compare/results/kerb_raised_nodes.json`). A share of routes rises with route length and is not a share of crossings. A bare `kerb:height` of 0.15 m or more also passes on the probe; that parsing is ORS issue #2293. Of the 15,599 crossing ends with no surveyed ramp within 5 m, 3,878 (24.9%) are tagged lowered or flush in OSM (`compare/results/kerb_crosscheck.json`). Some of these ramps were probably built after the survey, which dates mostly from 2018, and some OSM tags may be wrong. This project has not checked which. Unweaver and this repository's search agree on whether a route exists for all 1,354 requests, and routes both found differ by at most 6.4 m (`compare/results/unweaver_*.json`).

Matching. ORS wheelchair routes were matched to edges by the OSM way ids ORS returns, and since 2026-10-04 Valhalla routes by the way ids Valhalla's `trace_attributes` returns for each route (`compare/results/valhalla_way_ids.json`). Matched by way id, Valhalla's wheelchair routes take steps on 0.1% of routes city-wide, not the 3.8% geometry matching gave; a positive control finds steps on 6.0% of its foot routes in Brooklyn and 16.4% in Manhattan, and of the 1,193 foot routes where Valhalla's own edge use says steps the audit finds 1,156. The 10 wheelchair routes still flagged ride escalators, which Valhalla's wheelchair type does not exclude. ORS foot-walking routes are still matched by geometry only, because this run enabled ORS's `OsmId` storage for the wheelchair profile only, so their rows are not verified by the way-id method.

The imagery check of disagreements (`compare/disagreements/`) rated 48 sheets over March 2024 imagery, 12 for each of four causes. The fifth cause, rule (203 disagreements in the five boroughs), was not sampled: a rule disagreement has no point on the ORS route that this profile refuses, so a sheet has nothing to mark (`compare/disagreements/code/sheets.py` keeps only disagreements with a marked point). Every result in `compare/disagreements/score.json` is below. Counts are from the primary rating of all 48 sheets, and the intervals are exact 95% binomial intervals computed on 2026-10-03.

## Two sets of reachability numbers

Section 4 and section 6 give different wheelchair shares for the same 2,000 seeded pairs per borough:

| | Brooklyn | Queens | Manhattan | Bronx | Staten Island |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Node snapping (`reachability/reach.json`) | 84.8% | 63.0% | 53.0% | 43.7% | 43.1% |
| Nearest-edge snapping (`compare/results/comparison.json`) | 91.2% | 67.8% | 64.0% | 50.8% | 50.3% |

Both are correct for their method. `reach.json` starts each trip at the drawn graph node itself. That node can sit on an edge the wheelchair profile cannot use, or on a small piece of network cut off from the rest, and then the pair fails. The comparison does what the reference engines do with a coordinate: it moves each end to the nearest point on an edge the profile can use, on a network of at least 200 edges (`MIN_NETWORK_EDGES` in `compare/graph.py`, the size below which GraphHopper, inside ORS, drops a network). Run with node snapping, the comparison code reproduces `reach.json`'s counts exactly (1,696, 1,259, 1,060, 874 and 861 of 2,000). Edge snapping raises the shares by 4.8 to 11.0 points, most in Manhattan. Use the node-snapped figures to compare one version of the graph with another, since v0.3.2 was measured that way. Use the edge-snapped figures beside other routers, since that is how they treat a trip end. The same difference gives 94.0% against 94.8% for walking in Brooklyn and 3,769 m against 3,776 m for the Brooklyn Bridge route.

## What this evidence does not show

- This project checked nothing on the ground. Every statement about a ramp rests on the DOT ramp survey, which a contractor (Cyclomedia) collected for NYC DOT from vehicle-mounted street-level imagery and LiDAR, with records from March 2017 to January 2020, mostly 2018. No error rate for that survey is published. Every statement about slope rests on the 2017 LiDAR.
- The raters were Anthropic Claude model instances following written protocols, not people, and the exact model versions were not recorded. No person has looked at any of the 48 disagreement sheets, the 200 crossing sheets or the route images as a rater, so nothing measures the model's accuracy against human judgement. Agreement between instances measures consistency, not accuracy.
- The crossing rule's false pass rate is 1.2% with a 95% interval of 0.15% to 4.3% (2 of 166). 200 crossings cannot bound it more tightly. Seventeen rules were scored on the one sample.
- The crossing rule's recall is measured only against ramps that are in the survey. It cannot detect a crossing refused because the survey has no ramp there, and 24.9% of crossing ends with no surveyed ramp within 5 m are tagged lowered or flush in OSM. Some of those ramps were probably built after the survey, which dates mostly from 2018, and some OSM tags may be wrong. This project has not checked which.
- Imagery shows where a ramp is and almost never whether it is there: it showed a ramp directly at 9 of 400 crossing ends. The rating tests which crossing a surveyed ramp serves; existence rests on the vehicle-based survey. At 8 of 11 kerb disagreement places a rater could not tell.
- The imagery check of disagreements confirms the connectivity label only. Raters placed 2 of 12 structure sheets on a structure, incline cannot be judged from above, and the rule cause was not sampled.
- The landmark route images were drawn over NY State imagery at about 30 cm and rated by a language-model instance. They show that a route lies on sidewalks, crossings and decks, not whether a ramp is there. Those images are not published.
- The structure routes that fail mostly fail where this pipeline's height estimate jumps across one short edge at the foot of a ramp; on the Manhattan Bridge a 236 m deck edge also reads 11.1% down. The steepness comes from the pipeline's LiDAR heights, not from OSM, and OSM's geometry there is valid. The fix is in the incline method. The High Line entrances read as near-vertical because the pipeline drops OSM node tags: at 30th Street OSM maps the lift as `highway=elevator`, `wheelchair=yes` on node 2823833584 (`reachability/hand_check.md`).
- The 2014 comparison covers nodes whose height came from 2017. The 45 nodes on spans over open water have no second survey and were read for plausibility only (the Brooklyn Bridge deck at 41 to 45 m).
- Structures newer than May 2017 carry the height the survey saw then (LaGuardia, the new Kosciuszko span).
- The comparison's search is a re-implementation. It was run against Unweaver itself in a container and agreed on all 1,354 requests. City-wide, Unweaver was sampled at 40 pairs per area, since a query takes seconds to minutes.
- ORS and Valhalla ran without elevation data, and Valhalla with its default sidewalk weighting. A Valhalla run with elevation tiles was not done.
- ORS foot-walking routes were matched to edges by geometry, not way ids (this run enabled the way-id storage for the wheelchair profile only), so their steps, roadway and incline rows are less certain than the ORS wheelchair and Valhalla rows.
- "Roadway" counts street centrelines, including those where OSM tags a sidewalk on the street. How much of it is on such centrelines was not measured.
- Arm B was converted before the coincident-ways fix and lacks 286 of 1,437,901 ways. Arm C was rebuilt after it. The effect on arm B's shares was not measured.
- 387 pairs across all sets are routed by this graph and not by ORS on the same edges (arm C). Rough surfaces explain 37. The likely cause is where ORS snaps; this was not verified.
- Of the arm C routes that still differ, "1,319 of 1,390 use a stretch over the incline limits" covers only the different-route rows of the six random samples (`compare/results/arm_c_residual.json`).
- The "over the incline limits" audit counts any edge, short ones included. How much of it is LiDAR noise was not measured.
- The share of routes that pass over a `kerb=raised` node is not a share of crossings and rises with route length. Per crossing end, OSM tags 4.3% of the ends with no surveyed ramp and 0.2% of the ends with one `kerb=raised` (`compare/results/kerb_crosscheck.json`).
- This graph has no ferry edges. The comparison's shares of routes found include 349 Manhattan pairs and 698 city-wide pairs that no walking route inside the city can serve; `compare/results/tables.md` gives the shares with those pairs set apart. The engines were not rerun with ferries avoided, so how many of their other ferry routes had a walking alternative is not known.
- Of the 2,806 arm B strict routes that the audit flags for a crossing with no surveyed ramp, 146 were not classified beyond the finding that the crossing pieces they use carry no raised kerb.
- What Valhalla's wheelchair type excludes and does not exclude is stated from its 3.9.0 source and from the tables. It was not tested on a fixture as ORS was.
- The disagreement sample was drawn before the snapping fix. Causes depend on ORS's route, so they did not move, but the sheets were not redrawn.
- The steps count looks up ORS's way ids in the OSM tags of the ways this graph uses. 0.9% of the ids on the reference routes are not in that table, so a steps way among them would be missed (`compare/results/osm_tag_summary.json`).
- The behaviours of ORS stated here are as observed in v10.0.1 on the tag probe fixture, or read in its source where the text says so. Other versions may differ.
- The demo was checked by tools (axe-core and pa11y), not by a screen-reader user.
- The per-edge bridge blockers in `blocked_on_routes.json` are from the final build (commit `2442db2`). `reachability/hand_check.md` was written on the build at `6d7cc72`, before the George Washington Bridge tower fix in `2442db2`, and a dated note in it gives the differences.
