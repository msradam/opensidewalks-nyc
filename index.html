Everything drawn on the map is also given as text on this page: routes under Routes, the network under Network in numbers and every corner's ramps under Curb ramps. With the map focused, the arrow keys move it and the plus and minus keys zoom.

Show on the map

Streets, sidewalks and crossings: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL). Curb ramps: NYC DOT. Sidewalk width: NYC planimetric survey. Incline: NYC 2017 LiDAR. Full credits are under Sources.

Routes

Each trip below was computed three ways on the same OpenStreetMap extract of 1 October 2026: by this graph's wheelchair profile, by OpenRouteService's wheelchair profile on plain OpenStreetMap data, and by OpenRouteService's ordinary walking profile. The trips run from public housing developments, senior centres and subway entrances to clinics, schools, libraries and cooling sites, all taken from public city datasets.

Network in numbers

The same network the map draws, as counts. Sidewalk width is the mapped strip between kerb and property line from the city's planimetric survey, which is wider than the clear path left by poles, trees and stoops. Incline comes from the city's 2017 airborne LiDAR.

Curb ramps

Ramp positions and measurements come from NYC DOT's pedestrian ramp survey of April 2018 to October 2019. Nobody on this project has checked them with a level on the ramp. The status shown is DOT's own published assessment of that survey. DOT's caveat applies to all of it:

Please note that measurements shown are not indicative of whether a particular ramp is compliant with design and construction standards pursuant to the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA).

NYC DOT, description of the Pedestrian Ramp Locations dataset

What is verified and what is not

The planned field audit, and an invitation

The check that matters has not been done: people measuring ramps with a level and wheelchair users travelling the routes. A field audit of Brownsville is planned and has no partner yet. In outline it covers about 70 corners (roughly 125 ramps, measured for slope, lip, width and warning surface), 40 block faces (clear width, surface and slope) and 12 trips, each travelled twice by wheelchair users who judge whether a route is passable. The sample is drawn at random before anyone goes out, the people measuring do not see the survey's values, and the result is published whatever it shows.

Disability organisations and wheelchair users in Brooklyn are invited to take part, as co-researchers who set the pass and fail criteria, not as subjects. We are seeking funding to pay co-researchers. If you or your organisation would consider it, or if you can see from this page that something is wrong, please open an issue on the project's issue tracker.
