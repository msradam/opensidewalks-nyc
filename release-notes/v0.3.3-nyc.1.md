# v0.3.3-nyc.1

A pedestrian graph of New York City in the OpenSidewalks Schema v0.3. 4,068,058 features from the Geofabrik OpenStreetMap extract of 2026-10-01, passing `python-osw-validation` 0.5.0 with zero errors (a check of form, not of the schema's topology rules), built in one run at commit `2442db2`. 0.3.3-nyc.1 is this dataset's own version; it is not a schema version.

Read the [Disclaimer](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc#disclaimer) before using the data. It is also at the end of these notes. [Demo](https://msradam.github.io/opensidewalks-nyc/) · [README](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc#readme) · [Issues](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/issues)

## What is in it

- Sidewalks, crossings, footways, steps and street edges, each directed, with `incline` signed in the direction of travel. Both directions of every segment are stored, so do not add reverse edges.
- 217,679 curb ramps from the NYC DOT ramp survey, which a contractor (Cyclomedia) collected for DOT from vehicle-mounted street-level imagery and LiDAR, with records captured March 2017 to January 2020, mostly in 2018. Each carries the survey's slopes and warning surface status. 181,499 sit on the graph.
- Incline estimated from the 2017 LiDAR terrain model at 2 m. On bridges and elevated ways, 14,402 nodes take the deck height from the LiDAR point clouds instead of the ground below.
- Sidewalk widths estimated from the NYC planimetric polygons on 818,622 edges.

## Known limits

- The ramp data says a ramp was there when the imagery was captured, mostly in 2018, not that it is usable today. DOT says the data does not establish ADA compliance.
- Share of 2,000 random pairs per borough that the wheelchair profile can route, with each end at the graph node it was drawn at (node snapping): 84.8% Brooklyn, 63.0% Queens, 53.0% Manhattan, 43.7% Bronx, 43.1% Staten Island. The README's router comparison snaps each end to the nearest edge instead, which gives 91.2%, 67.8%, 64.0%, 50.8% and 50.3%.
- Taking the profile's constraints away one at a time, most of the loss in the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island comes from streets whose sidewalks OSM maps as `sidewalk=*` tags on the street. That is a valid OSM scheme, and this pipeline does not read it, so those streets have no sidewalk edge. In Brooklyn crossings with no surveyed ramp cost the most, and in Manhattan incline does.
- The wheelchair profile does not cross the Manhattan, Queensboro, Williamsburg or Pulaski bridges. On the Williamsburg, Queensboro and Pulaski bridges, this pipeline's height estimate jumps across one short edge where a ramp meets the ground, and the Queensboro's outer roadway also reads 8.8% over 105 m. On the Manhattan Bridge a 56.9 m ramp edge reads 9.7% and a 236 m deck edge reads 11.1% down. Where a short edge jumps, the fix is in the incline method, not in OpenStreetMap. Staten Island has no pedestrian link to the other boroughs.
- The 5 m ramp to crossing rule was checked on 200 crossings over aerial imagery by language-model raters, not by people and not on site.
- Six curb nodes carry counter slopes over 100% and two edges carry `width=0.0`. Treat them as unmeasured.
- The README lists the places where the data departs from the OpenSidewalks Schema.

Evidence for every number: [`evaluation/`](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/tree/main/evaluation).

## Disclaimer

OpenSidewalks NYC is an experimental dataset. This project has not checked any of it on the ground. Do not use it to tell anyone that a route is accessible, and do not rely on it, or on an app built from it, to plan a trip.

The curb ramp survey shows that a ramp was there when it was captured, mostly in 2018, not that it is usable today. Incline and width are estimates.
