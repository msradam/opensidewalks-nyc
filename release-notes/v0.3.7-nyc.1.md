# v0.3.7-nyc.1

OpenSidewalks NYC is a data file of New York City's sidewalks, street crossings, plazas and curb ramps, for researchers and developers. It is not a trip planner. It is an independent project that uses the OpenSidewalks Schema v0.3. This release has 3,995,589 features from the Geofabrik OpenStreetMap extract of 2026-10-01, the same extract as v0.3.3 to v0.3.6, and passes `python-osw-validation` 0.5.0 with zero errors (a check of form, not of the schema's topology rules). Built in one run from commit `c442658`. 0.3.7-nyc.1 is this dataset's own version; it is not a schema version.

Read the [Disclaimer](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc#disclaimer) before using the data. It is also at the end of these notes. [Demo](https://msradam.github.io/opensidewalks-nyc/) · [README](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc#readme) · [Contributing](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md) · [Issues](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/issues)

## What is in it

- Sidewalks, crossings, footways, pedestrian roads, steps and street edges, each directed, with `incline` signed in the direction of travel. Both directions of every segment are stored, so do not add reverse edges.
- 2,201 Pedestrian Zones: plazas and other pedestrian areas, each a Polygon whose `_w_id` lists the Nodes of its outline.
- 217,679 curb ramps from the NYC DOT ramp survey, which a contractor (Cyclomedia) collected for DOT from vehicle-mounted street-level imagery and LiDAR, with records captured March 2017 to January 2020, mostly in 2018. Each carries the survey's slopes, its warning surface status and DOT's own condition value. 184,939 sit on the graph, 166,310 of them on the end of the crossing they serve.
- 22,238 curb nodes from OpenStreetMap's own `kerb` tags (14,392 lowered, 4,903 generic, 1,610 flush, 1,220 raised, 113 rolled), and 137 elevator nodes.
- A street edge's `ext:sidewalk` says what OpenStreetMap says about the street's sidewalks, on 344,718 of 950,416 street edges.
- Incline estimated from the 2017 LiDAR terrain model at 2 m. On bridges and elevated ways, 14,465 nodes take the height of the deck instead of the ground below.
- 1,538 nodes have no `ext:elevation_m` (tunnels, structures with no deck height, open water in the terrain model). 2,042 Edges carry `ext:incline_unknown=yes` and no `incline`, because their two end heights give a grade no walkway is built at.
- Sidewalk widths estimated from the NYC planimetric polygons on 818,686 edges.

## What changed since v0.3.6

- A street carries what OpenStreetMap says about its sidewalks. OSM records a sidewalk either as its own way or as a tag on the street (`sidewalk=both`, `left`, `right`, `yes`, `no` or `separate`, or per side as `sidewalk:left`, `sidewalk:right` and `sidewalk:both`). Up to v0.3.6 the pipeline read only the first form. Now a street edge with either form carries `ext:sidewalk`, folded to one value, with the raw per-side values kept in `ext:sidewalk_left` and `ext:sidewalk_right`. The street stays a street edge: no sidewalk line is drawn from it, because an offset line would be a guess that joins nothing and would show as a surveyed sidewalk. The routing layer's wheelchair profile walks a street tagged as having a sidewalk, as a stand-in for the sidewalk beside it, and refuses one tagged `no` or `separate` or untagged, as before. A route along such a street passes its intersections with no kerb check, because the dataset has no crossing there.
- That change reaches fewer streets than v0.3.6's notes implied. They said most of the wheelchair profile's losses in the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island came from streets whose sidewalks OSM maps as tags. A count of the pinned extract shows otherwise: of the 108,703 street ways the filter keeps inside the city's bounding box, 104,555 carry no `sidewalk=*` tag, 2,519 say `separate`, 1,383 say `both`, `left`, `right` or `yes`, and the per-side form (which 26,938 ways use, almost all among the 104,555) mostly says `separate` or `no`, with about 300 saying `yes` on a side. So about 1,700 streets have a sidewalk that exists only as a tag, and the gap in those boroughs is streets with no sidewalk mapped in any form. The reachability figures below show the size of the gain.
- A surveyed curb ramp sits on the end of the crossing it serves. Up to v0.3.6 each ramp snapped to the nearest pedestrian vertex within 5 m, which was as often a sidewalk vertex beside the crossing as the crossing's own end: 55,347 of the 181,499 attached ramps (30%) sat on no crossing. Now a ramp goes to a crossing end within 5 m when there is one (the node where a crossing meets a sidewalk, footway or plaza outline; each end takes its nearest ramp), and only otherwise to the nearest vertex of any kind. 166,310 of 184,939 attached ramps (89.9%) now sit on a crossing end and 16,159 on no crossing. No ramp's survey values changed, and the routing layer's 5 m rule is unchanged.
- OpenStreetMap's own kerb nodes are carried. A node tagged `barrier=kerb`, or `kerb=lowered`, `raised`, `flush` or `rolled`, is written as the schema's Curb Ramp, Raised Curb, Flush Curb, Rolled Curb or Generic Curb, with `tactile_paving` where OSM's value is one the schema lists: 22,238 nodes (14,392 lowered, 4,903 generic, 1,610 flush, 1,220 raised, 113 rolled). Where a surveyed ramp lands on an OSM kerb node (85,931 nodes), the node carries the survey's `kerb` and `tactile_paving` and OSM's stay beside them in `ext:osm_kerb` and `ext:osm_tactile_paving`, whether or not they agree; 4,383 of them disagree about the kerb. The routing layer counts a lowered or flush kerb as one a wheelchair can take and a raised or generic one as not, whichever source it came from.
- Elevators are carried. An OSM `highway=elevator` node is a bare node with `ext:osm_highway=elevator` (137 nodes), and the edges at one have no `incline` and no `ext:incline_unknown`, because an elevator joins two levels by machine. In v0.3.6, 14 elevator nodes sat on an edge marked unknown, the High Line's among them, and the wheelchair profile refused them. OSM's elevator ways (57 inside the city) are the closed outlines of elevator shafts and are not read.
- Greenways mapped as cycleways with no `foot` tag are kept. OpenStreetMap's access defaults for the United States give `foot=yes` on a cycleway and on a track, and up to v0.3.6 the pipeline kept either only with a `foot` tag of `yes`, `designated` or `permissive`, which left out 627 cycleway ways and 246 tracks. Now a cycleway or track with no `foot` tag is kept, with one exception: a cycleway tagged `oneway=yes` and no `foot` tag stays out, because in New York that is a bike lane beside the roadway, which runs through intersections with no crossing and has the sidewalk beside it, while a two-way one is a shared path or a greenway. 8,792 edges were added this way; 218 one-way cycleway ways stay out, a few of them with greenway names, and nobody has checked a sample of them.
- The GraphML root holds the OpenStreetMap extract's details (`osmExtract`) as a JSON string, not a Python dictionary printed as text, and the routing JSON's description names OpenSidewalks NYC.
- The repository has a CONTRIBUTING file, an issue template for data errors (where, what is wrong, how you know), a changelog, and a Contributing section in the README that says how mappers can help in OpenStreetMap without tagging for this project.

## What did not change

Every feature of v0.3.6 is in v0.3.7 with the same geometry and the same values, except where the changes above touch it: geometry is the same on 2,797,528 of the 2,797,534 shared edges (the other six, four road edges and two footways, follow three endpoints the near-miss merge now joins differently, each moved 1.4 to 2.0 m, in Brooklyn, Queens and Lower Manhattan), incline is the same on 2,795,142 shared edges and differs on 492 at elevators, 1,884 within three hops of a new edge or a moved endpoint (heights are smoothed over short edges, so a new neighbour moves them), the six at that endpoint, and 10 by at most 0.0047 that no change explains, and every one of the 217,679 ramps carries the same survey fields. 48,630 ramps changed node, so a ramp is matched by `ext:ramp_id`, not by node id. `compare_v036.json` in `evaluation/v0.3.7/` lists every difference by its cause. Widths, surfaces, names, crossing markings and the survey fields of every ramp are unchanged; 2,199 of the 2,201 zones are unchanged, and two have one outline vertex moved about 1 m onto a different node at one of those endpoints.

## Reachability

On the same 2,000 node pairs per borough that were drawn on v0.3.4 (`evaluation/v0.3.5/pairs_v0.3.4.json`), with each end at the graph node it was drawn at, the wheelchair profile routes:

| Pairs routed of 2,000 | v0.3.6 | v0.3.7 | Lost | Gained |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brooklyn | 1,694 (84.7%) | 1,760 (88.0%) | 0 | 66 |
| Queens | 1,258 (62.9%) | 1,311 (65.6%) | 0 | 53 |
| Manhattan | 1,056 (52.8%) | 1,097 (54.9%) | 0 | 41 |
| Bronx | 872 (43.6%) | 933 (46.7%) | 0 | 61 |
| Staten Island | 861 (43.1%) | 885 (44.3%) | 0 | 24 |
| Ends anywhere in the city | 803 | 848 | 2 | 47 |

The wheelchair profile gains routes in every borough and loses two, both pairs with ends anywhere in the city whose end sits on a crossing that counted a surveyed ramp within 5 m in v0.3.6 and does not now, because that ramp moved onto the end of the crossing it serves, more than 5 m away. The gains come from three of the changes together: streets tagged as having a sidewalk, which the profile now walks; OpenStreetMap's lowered and flush kerbs, which count at a crossing end as a surveyed ramp does; and elevators and the cycleways and tracks kept under the United States default. The run on the rehearsal build, which carried no OSM node tags, gained 14, 32, 1, 37 and 3 pairs in Brooklyn, Queens, Manhattan, the Bronx and Staten Island, so most of the gain is OSM's kerbs. The wheelchair profile with no incline limit gains 67, 50, 46, 100 and 76 and loses none; the walking profile (no street centrelines) gains 5, 1, 0, 1 and 3, which are the new cycleway and track edges; the profile that walks every edge gains 8, 2, 0, 2 and 0. The routing layer calls 387,802 of 437,940 crossing edges ramped, against 387,442 of 437,510 in v0.3.6.

## Known limits

- The ramp data says a ramp was there when the imagery was captured, mostly in 2018, not that it is usable today. DOT says the data does not establish ADA compliance.
- Much of the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island has streets with no sidewalk mapped in OpenStreetMap in any form, and the wheelchair profile has no route there. A street tagged as having a sidewalk is walked with no kerb check at its intersections. The graph has no ferry edges, so Governors Island, Liberty Island and Ellis Island are cut off, and Staten Island has no pedestrian link to the other boroughs.
- 16,159 attached ramps are on no crossing, because no crossing end lies within 5 m of them; the routing layer counts a ramp within 5 m of a crossing end for this reason.
- The one-way cycleway rule has not been checked on a sample: 218 ways stay out, and some carry greenway names.
- Where OSM and the survey disagree about a kerb (4,383 nodes, almost all OSM `flush` against the survey's `lowered`), the file carries both values and does not say which is right.
- The wheelchair profile does not cross the Manhattan, Queensboro, Williamsburg or Pulaski bridges, because the height estimate jumps across short ramp edges or reads steep deck edges (measured on v0.3.3). Of the 17 landmark routes, the wheelchair profile now finds one it did not: between the two ends of the High Line it finds a 1,706 m route, 17 m longer than the walker's, on the Tenth Avenue sidewalks (68% sidewalk, 17% footway, 15% crossing), not on the High Line's deck; the elevator at 30th Street is carried, but the route the profile picks still stays at street level. Three bridge detours shortened: Pulaski from 14,119 m to 3,691 m, Macombs Dam from 8,691 m to 4,842 m (23% of it on streets tagged as having a sidewalk) and Queensboro from 24,131 m to 19,186 m, and nine street-level routes shortened by 38 m to 266 m. The Manhattan Bridge and the Wards Island footbridge still have no wheelchair route (`evaluation/v0.3.7/reach.json`).
- An Edge marked `ext:incline_unknown=yes` has end heights that disagree by more than any walkway climbs, and the wheelchair profile refuses it. An Edge with no `incline` and no mark was not measured (a tunnel, an elevator, an end with no height) and still passes.
- The 5 m ramp to crossing rule was checked on 200 crossings over aerial imagery by language-model raters, not by people and not on site.
- The README lists the places where the data still departs from the OpenSidewalks Schema.

## Evidence

Each claim these notes make about v0.3.7 has a file in [`evaluation/v0.3.7/`](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/tree/main/evaluation/v0.3.7):

- `validator.json`: the `python-osw-validation` 0.5.0 result (`is_valid: true`, no errors) with the SHA-256 of the ZIP it read, which is the `nyc-osw-osw-split.zip` line of `SHA256SUMS`.
- `checks.json`: the checks the validator does not make, now with `ramp_position`, `osm_kerbs`, `elevators`, `street_sidewalk_tags` and `shared_paths` blocks. `checks_v0.3.6.json` is the same checks run on v0.3.6.
- `compare_v036.json`: every feature of v0.3.6 against v0.3.7, each difference grouped by the change that caused it (`code/compare_v036.py`).
- `reach_same_pairs_v0.3.7.json`: reachability on the pairs drawn for v0.3.4, made by `code/reach_same_pairs.py`. `reach.json` draws its pairs afresh and routes the landmark pairs.
- `bridges.json`: the 23 bridges end to end.
- `build.json` and `snap_report.json`: the commit, the times, the memory, the extract's checksum and the endpoint snap.
- `SHA256SUMS`: the checksums of the release assets.

The imagery ratings and the router comparison in [`evaluation/`](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc/tree/main/evaluation) were run on v0.3.3 and were not repeated.

## Assets

`nyc-osw.geojson.gz` (canonical), `nyc-osw.fgb`, `nyc-osw.graphml.gz`, `nyc-osw-undirected.graphml.gz`, `nyc-routing.json.gz`, `nyc-osw-{MN,BK,QN,BX,SI}.geojson.gz`, `nyc-osw-osw-split.zip` (validator input: nodes, edges and zones), `nyc-gapfill-sidewalks.geojson` (candidate sidewalk centerlines from the city's planimetric polygons where OSM has no separate sidewalk way, not part of the graph), `evaluation-sheets.zip` (the 248 imagery sheets the language-model raters rated, unchanged since v0.3.4) and `SHA256SUMS`, which lists every one of them.

The GraphML files, the routing JSON and the FlatGeobuf were all made from the build's output with the build commit's code. In the GraphML files and the routing JSON, each Pedestrian Zone appears as the edges a person can walk across it. Every GraphML edge carries `length_m`. In the FlatGeobuf, a zone's `_w_id` is a JSON array in a text field.

## Disclaimer

OpenSidewalks NYC is an experimental dataset. This project has not checked any of it on the ground. Do not use it to tell anyone that a route is accessible, and do not rely on it, or on an app built from it, to plan a trip.

The curb ramp survey shows that a ramp was there when it was captured, mostly in 2018, not that it is usable today. Incline and width are estimates, not measurements.

Language models drafted most of the code and documentation and did the imagery ratings. [How This Was Made](https://github.com/msradam/opensidewalks-nyc#how-this-was-made) says how they were used.
